Clearlake Takes Full Control of Chelsea as Todd Boehly Exits

Todd Boehly and Mark Walter are selling their stakes for a reported £950m, leaving Clearlake in charge with Chelsea’s stadium question still unresolved.

James Callan18 Sep 20267 min read

Clearlake Capital will take full control of Chelsea after agreeing to buy Todd Boehly and Mark Walter out of the club, bringing Boehly’s four-year spell as chairman to an end.

Chelsea have confirmed the ownership change, with Hansjörg Wyss remaining involved. The Financial Times reports that Boehly and Walter will receive around £950 million in cash for their combined 25% stake, valuing Chelsea at roughly £5 billion including debt. The deal is expected to be completed before the end of the year.

For all the size of that number, Chelsea aren’t suddenly becoming a different club.

Clearlake has held 61.5% since the 2022 takeover and Behdad Eghbali has already become one of the most influential figures around the football operation. Chelsea have said there’ll be no change to the club’s day-to-day leadership or sporting strategy, while Boehly’s role had already become much smaller than it was during those first few months when he briefly doubled up as interim sporting director and Chelsea seemed determined to find out how many signings one transfer window could physically take.

The real change is at the top.

Despite owning most of Chelsea, Clearlake originally shared equal governance and joint control with Boehly. By 2024, that relationship had deteriorated badly enough for both sides to explore ways of ending it, with the future of Stamford Bridge becoming one of the clearest areas where they didn’t see things the same way.

Chelsea’s new owners paid £2.5 billion for the club in 2022 and committed another £1.75 billion to future investment, with Stamford Bridge specifically included. Four years later, there still isn’t a final answer on what happens to the ground.

That’s partly because Stamford Bridge is one of the more awkward major stadium sites in European football.

Chelsea have played there since the club was founded in 1905, but a capacity of just over 40,000 leaves them well behind several of the Premier League clubs they’re competing with commercially. Expanding it sounds easy enough until you look at what’s actually around it.

Railway lines run directly beside the stadium, which limits how far Chelsea can push development outwards. Going significantly higher isn’t straightforward either because Stamford Bridge sits within the protected viewing corridor from King Henry VIII’s Mound in Richmond Park towards St Paul’s Cathedral.

That doesn’t mean Chelsea simply can’t build upwards, but it does place a legal height threshold on development in the corridor. The abandoned Abramovich-era redevelopment had to be designed around it and was planned to sit roughly five metres below the permitted limit.

So the obvious supporter suggestion of “just stick another tier on it” quickly runs into railway infrastructure, protected views of St Paul’s and west London planning law.

Chelsea have created a bit more room by buying the neighbouring Stoll site, but rebuilding on the existing footprint would still be enormously complicated and could force the club away from Stamford Bridge for several seasons.

That’s why Earl’s Court has kept coming back into the conversation.

Chelsea have explored the site as a possible new home, although the existing regeneration plans don’t include a football stadium. Moving would bring another very Chelsea complication too. Chelsea Pitch Owners owns the Stamford Bridge freehold and holds rights connected to the Chelsea FC name, an arrangement created in the 1990s to protect the club’s future at its home.

Boehly himself linked the stadium issue to the future of the ownership group in 2025, acknowledging that the owners could eventually go separate ways if they couldn’t agree on Chelsea’s long-term home.

The split has now happened, but there’s far less reason to expect the same kind of change on the football side.

Boehly was the public face of Chelsea’s early spending, but the club has since built a much larger sporting structure around Paul Winstanley and Laurence Stewart. The approach is fairly recognisable now: target young players, secure them on long contracts, protect their value and keep trading aggressively around the squad.

There have been periods when Chelsea’s player list looked like somebody had accidentally merged the first team with a Football Manager save, but there’s much more structure behind it now than there was during those first frantic windows.

The results have improved too. Chelsea have returned to the Champions League places, won the Conference League and then lifted the expanded Club World Cup.

The Strasbourg relationship fits into the same setup. BlueCo bought the Ligue 1 club in 2023 and the link has become increasingly visible through players, loans and staff, although UEFA’s multi-club ownership rules still place limits on shared influence when connected clubs qualify for the same European competitions.

Most of the football operation Clearlake wants is already there.

The question that hasn’t gone away is Stamford Bridge.

For supporters, that isn’t just another line on an investment plan. It’s been Chelsea’s home for the club’s entire history, and through Chelsea Pitch Owners it’s a ground fans quite literally helped protect when the club’s future there wasn’t always secure.

Chelsea need more from it. Just over 40,000 seats isn’t enough for a club trying to compete commercially with the biggest sides in England, but rebuilding it has never been as simple as getting the builders in.

The ownership dispute might be finished, but Chelsea supporters are still waiting to find out whether the next version of their club’s home means rebuilding the ground they’ve known for generations or eventually leaving it behind.

For all the money changing hands this week, that’s probably the part they’ll care about most.
