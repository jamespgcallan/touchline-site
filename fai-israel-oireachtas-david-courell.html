FAI to Defend Israel Fixture Decision Before Oireachtas Committee

David Courell faces questions over Gavin Bazunu, the player vote and how the FAI handled an extraordinary week around the Ireland squad.

James Callan30 Sep 20267 min read

The FAI’s handling of the Republic of Ireland’s Nations League fixtures against Israel moves to Leinster House on Wednesday, with chief executive David Courell and three other senior figures due to explain how the association reached its decision to play and how that decision was handled once the Ireland squad assembled.

Courell will appear before the Oireachtas sports committee alongside FAI president Paul Cooke, chairman Tony Keohane and director of football John Martin. Safeguarding and wider governance are also on the agenda, but after several days of player meetings, political criticism and questions around Gavin Bazunu’s withdrawal, the senior team is likely to take up a fair amount of the oxygen in the room.

The FAI’s defence has already been laid out. In prepared evidence, Courell will argue that the association is required to operate within UEFA rules and that fulfilling the fixtures should not be interpreted as support for Israel’s continued participation in European competition.

“As a UEFA member and competition participant, however, the FAI is bound by the applicable rules and obligations.”

There was also a financial calculation behind the decision. FAI members were warned that refusing to fulfil the fixtures could cost the association at least €5.5 million, before any additional disciplinary or longer-term consequences were considered. The General Assembly ultimately backed the board’s position by 75 votes to 32, with three abstentions.

For an association that has spent years trying to repair its finances, €5.5 million is hardly the sort of figure you lose down the back of a sofa. The less certain part is what might have come after it. Cooke acknowledged following Sunday’s game that the FAI still did not know the full extent of any additional sanctions UEFA could have imposed.

That gives the committee a useful line of questioning. Was the €5.5 million exposure established, estimated or part of a wider worst-case scenario? What other sporting or financial penalties were being considered? And how much certainty did the board actually have when it asked members to approve the decision?

The situation then became much more complicated once the players arrived.

Gavin Bazunu told manager Heimir Hallgrímsson that he did not feel able to take part in the first Israel fixture and withdrew from the squad. It subsequently emerged that the goalkeeper had not been among the group of roughly 30 internationals contacted during the FAI’s earlier consultation with players.

Hallgrímsson later said he had spoken directly to the outfield players but not the goalkeepers. Once everyone was together in Debrecen, what should have been the final preparation for a Nations League match instead became hours of discussion about whether the players were comfortable taking part.

The scheduled pre-match press conference was delayed by more than five hours while those conversations continued. Two votes were held within the squad, with reporting from The 42 placing the final margin at either 14-8 or 15-7 in favour of playing. Bazunu was not involved.

Courell described the outcome rather more broadly.

“A significant majority.”

That wording will almost certainly attract attention on Wednesday because the vote itself only tells part of the story. The FAI had already decided to fulfil the fixture. Its members had backed that position. Yet by the eve of the match, the players were still being asked to work through one of the most difficult decisions an international squad could reasonably face.

When the football finally arrived, Ireland produced their best result of the Nations League campaign. Troy Parrott, Adam Idah and Jack Moylan scored before half-time as Hallgrímsson’s side won 3-0 in Debrecen and picked up their first points following the opening defeat in Kosovo.

Even then, it was never going to look like an ordinary international night. Ireland’s players wore black armbands, bowed their heads during the Israeli anthem and did not shake hands with their opponents before kick-off.

Those details matter because they show just how far the issue had travelled from the original boardroom decision. What began with UEFA regulations and the financial consequences of refusing to play eventually reached the dressing room, where players had to decide how they wanted to respond themselves.

Wednesday’s hearing should give the FAI an opportunity to explain that sequence properly. Why was Bazunu not included in the initial consultation? What exactly were players told before joining camp? What changed once the squad gathered? And at what point did an organisational decision become something the players themselves were being asked to settle?

Political pressure has increased in the meantime. Independent TD Ruth Coppinger has called for Courell and the FAI board to resign, while Social Democrats TD Daniel Ennis has focused criticism on the treatment of the senior squad.

Neither intervention changes the FAI’s position, but both add another layer to a hearing that was already going to cover considerably more than 90 minutes of football.

There is not much time for the dust to settle either. Ireland host Austria at the Aviva Stadium on Thursday before facing Israel again on Sunday 4 October.

“Host” is doing a little work there. Ireland’s designated home fixture against Israel will actually take place behind closed doors at the TSC Arena in Bačka Topola, Serbia.

By Sunday evening, Ireland could have played Israel twice in eight days, in two different countries, with an Oireachtas hearing squeezed in between.

The football calendar will keep moving regardless. Wednesday is the point where the FAI has to stop, sit down and explain how it all got this far.

If this one landed, the newsletter’s where the rest of it lives — two or three pieces a week, no noise.
