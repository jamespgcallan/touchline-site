Sligo Rovers Members Back Restructure That Could Bring Private Investment

The Showgrounds will remain in member hands, while a new football company could eventually allow Sligo Rovers to bring in outside investment.

James Callan25 Sep 20265 min read

Sligo Rovers members have overwhelmingly backed plans to restructure the club, opening the door to future private investment while keeping the Showgrounds and majority control in member hands.

There were 225 members at Thursday night's Special General Meeting, either in the room or joining online, with the proposal receiving near-unanimous support. It needed 75% approval to progress and will return to members on 14 October for a final vote.

For all the talk of investment, Sligo haven't actually sold anything. There is no investor lined up and the club says it isn't currently in discussions with one. Members have instead agreed to create a structure which would allow those conversations to happen in future.

A separate company would run the men's and women's senior teams and academy, initially owned entirely by the member-run Sligo Football and Sport Development Society. The Society would appoint its directors and retain majority control if a minority investor eventually came on board.

The Showgrounds won't be going with it. Its lease, which runs until June 2064, will remain with the Society alongside responsibility for the ground and its redevelopment. The football company would use the stadium rather than own it, protecting the home Rovers have occupied since their foundation in 1928.

And quite a home it could soon become. More than €22 million is set to be spent turning the Showgrounds into a 6,035-seat UEFA Category 3 stadium, with completion targeted for the club's centenary in 2028.

It leaves Sligo preparing for one of the biggest transformations in their history while having a much less glamorous conversation about how to pay for the football team that will eventually play there.

Rovers generated €2.72 million in income during 2025 and reduced their deficit to €45,000, down from €299,000 in 2023. The club still budgeted €2.9 million for this season, while members heard at April's AGM that cash flow had required an overdraft, loans from committee members and arrangements with suppliers.

Supporters are hardly sitting on their hands either. The Annual Draw has raised more than €150,000 in each of the last two years, while the Friends of Sligo Rovers 500 Club had passed €2 million raised by 2024. There aren't many professional clubs where volunteers selling draw tickets can still make a six-figure difference to the accounts. At Sligo, they can.

Private money has become increasingly common elsewhere in the League of Ireland, leaving Rovers trying to protect a model that has served them for generations while finding the money required to compete in a very different league.

Thursday night provided a nicely timed example of another way Sligo have traditionally found it. While members discussed funding the club's future, academy graduate Owen Elding was making his senior Republic of Ireland debut against Kosovo, triggering another payment from Hibernian following his €500,000 move in January.

Producing a player, selling him for half a million and getting another cheque when he plays for Ireland is good business. You just can't put "find another Owen Elding" into next year's budget and hope for the best.

If the restructure receives final approval on 14 October, Sligo will still need FAI approval to transfer their licence to the new company before it can operate from 2027. Only then could outside investment properly enter the conversation.

Until then, the Showgrounds stays with the members, the academy keeps producing players and the draw tickets probably aren't going anywhere.

After nearly a century of Sligo finding the money themselves, they're making room for the possibility that the next few million might have to come from somewhere else.

If this one landed, the newsletter’s where the rest of it lives — two or three pieces a week, no noise.
